## 2. Objective & Scope

This rubric defines the economic, inventory, and merchandising principles used to construct the **105 benchmark scenarios** across five commercial categories:
- `ELECTRONICS` (21 scenarios)
- `APPAREL` (21 scenarios)
- `HOME` (21 scenarios)
- `BEAUTY` (21 scenarios)
- `GROCERY` (21 scenarios)

The scenarios evaluate whether dynamic pricing and replenishment engines behave rationally under real-world retail conditions, including high-stress edge cases (zero inventory, extreme demand surges, tight margin constraints, and dead stock).

---

## 3. Directional Pricing Rubric

Every scenario assigns a ground-truth expected price direction: `INCREASE`, `DECREASE`, or `HOLD`.

| Scenario Archetype | Condition | Expected Direction | Economic & Merchandising Rationale |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Critical Stockout Risk** | `stockLevel < reorderThreshold` AND `demandVelocity > 0` | `INCREASE` | **Scarcity Rationing**: When inventory coverage is below replenishment lead time, increasing price dampens run-rate, prolongs on-shelf presence, and captures consumer willingness to pay before stockout. |
| **Surge Harvesting** | `demandVelocity >= 2.5 * categoryAverageVelocity` | `INCREASE` | **Demand Inelasticity**: Sudden viral or seasonal surges exhibit price insensitivity. A moderate price bump (+3% to +10%) maximizes gross margin without choking demand velocity. |
| **Equilibrium / Stable Demand** | `stockLevel >= reorderThreshold` AND `0.8 * avg <= demandVelocity <= 1.5 * avg` | `HOLD` | **Friction Minimization**: When supply and demand are in balance, unprompted price adjustments create friction, damage conversion rates, and alienate recurring buyers. |
| **Severe Overstock / Slow Mover** | `stockLevel > 4 * reorderThreshold` AND `demandVelocity < 0.5 * avg` | `HOLD` or `DECREASE` | **Carrying Cost Mitigation**: Excessive inventory incurs warehouse holding costs, working capital lockup, and obsolescence risk. Price should never increase; it should hold steady or discount moderately for clearance. |
| **Near-Cost Floor Constraint** | `currentPrice <= costPrice * 1.10` (Gross margin <= 10%) | `HOLD` or `INCREASE` | **Margin Floor Protection**: Discounting items priced near cost causes negative unit economics. Price must hold or increase to preserve solvency. |
| **Zero Inventory (Stockout)** | `stockLevel == 0` | `INCREASE` or `HOLD` | **Backorder Demand Rationing**: Raising price on out-of-stock items tempers backorder commitments; lowering price on out-of-stock items is irrational. |

## 4. Price Window & Midpoint Specification

For each scenario, the rubric specifies:
1. `expectedPrice`: The ideal economic price midpoint.
2. `minPrice`: The lower boundary of the acceptable price window.
3. `maxPrice`: The upper boundary of the acceptable price window.

### Mathematical Formulation of Price Windows

1. **Scarcity Markup (Inventory Low)**:
   $$\text{Coverage Ratio } C = \frac{\text{stockLevel}}{\max(1, \text{demandVelocity})}$$
   $$\text{Deficit Severity } S = \max\left(0, 1 - \frac{\text{stockLevel}}{\text{reorderThreshold}}\right)$$
   $$\text{Target Markup } \Delta P = \text{currentPrice} \times \min(0.15, 0.05 + 0.10 \times S)$$
   $$\text{expectedPrice} = \text{roundToCents}(\text{currentPrice} + \Delta P)$$
   $$\text{Window}: [\text{currentPrice} \times 1.02, \; \text{currentPrice} \times 1.20]$$

2. **Surge Markup (Demand Spike)**:
   $$\text{Surge Multiplier } M = \frac{\text{demandVelocity}}{\text{categoryAverageVelocity}}$$
   $$\text{Target Surge Markup } \Delta P = \text{currentPrice} \times \min(0.12, 0.02 + 0.02 \times \min(5, M))$$
   $$\text{expectedPrice} = \text{roundToCents}(\text{currentPrice} + \Delta P)$$
   $$\text{Window}: [\text{currentPrice} \times 1.02, \; \text{currentPrice} \times 1.15]$$

3. **Margin-Clamped Scenarios**:
   $$\text{Floor} = \max(\text{costPrice} \times (1 + \text{minMargin}), \; 0.5 \times \text{currentPrice})$$
   If $\text{expectedPrice} < \text{Floor}$, $\text{expectedPrice} = \text{Floor}$.
   $$\text{minPrice} \ge \text{Floor}$$

## 5. Reorder Quantity Rubric

The ground-truth reorder quantity reflects standard inventory control theory (safety stock + replenishment cycle demand):

$$\text{Cycle Stock } Q_c = \text{demandVelocity} \times \text{targetDaysOfSupply}$$
$$\text{Safety Stock } S_s = Z \times \sqrt{\text{leadTimeDays}} \times \sigma_{\text{daily}}$$
$$\text{Net Replenishment } Q_{\text{target}} = \max\left(0, \; (Q_c + S_s) - \text{stockLevel}\right)$$

### Acceptable Replenishment Windows

- For high-velocity items: $\text{minQty} = 0.70 \times Q_{\text{target}}$, $\text{maxQty} = 1.40 \times Q_{\text{target}}$
- For items with `stockLevel > 3 * reorderThreshold`: $\text{expectedQty} = 0$, $\text{maxQty} \le 15$ (Do not reorder into overstock)
- For zero-stock items: $\text{minQty} = \text{reorderThreshold} \times 1.5$ (Urgent replenishment buffer)

## 7. Compliance and Verification Rules

1. **Independence**: Evaluators must verify that rubric formulas are evaluated independently of `RuleBasedAdvisor.js` code constants.
2. **Determinism**: Benchmark runs must be executable deterministically offline without external API dependencies.
3. **Wilson Score Intervals**: Every agreement percentage must be reported with 95% Wilson confidence intervals.
4. **Paired Statistical Comparison**: Differences between rule and AI models must be evaluated using McNemar's paired test with continuity correction, reporting the two-tailed $p$-value and whether the null hypothesis (equivalent accuracy) is rejected at $\alpha = 0.05$.

## 8. Peer Review & Methodological Limitations (Second-Reader Assessment)

An informal second-reader review identified three foundational methodological limitations in this benchmark:

1. **Label Circularity on Inventory Replenishment**:
   The rubric for reorder quantity (Section 5) specifies target replenishment as:
   $$Q_{\text{target}} = \max(0, \; (\text{demandVelocity} \times \text{targetDaysOfSupply} + S_s) - \text{stockLevel})$$
   Because `DaysOfSupplyRuleAdvisor` implements the exact textbook order-up-to periodic-review policy ($L=7\text{d}, R=7\text{d}, S_s=3\text{d}$), its high accuracy (82.2% on held-out split) is fundamentally a demonstration of **formulaic self-agreement** with the rubric rather than an empirical validation against real-world commercial outcomes.

2. **Pricing Comparison is Effectively Two-Way, Not Three-Way**:
   `NaiveRule` and `DaysOfSupplyRule` implement identical pricing heuristics (10% increase on low stock, 5% increase on demand surge). Consequently, both rule advisors produce identical pricing predictions (68/73 = 93.2% window accuracy, 98.5% price proximity on held-out split). Pricing evaluation is therefore strictly a two-way comparison between the baseline heuristic and the AI advisor.

3. **Directional Triviality & Ceiling Effect**:
   A completely velocity-blind rule achieves 100% directional accuracy on the dataset. A 3-class decision (`INCREASE`, `DECREASE`, `HOLD`) on low-stock and demand-surge scenarios is trivially saturated by simple threshold checks. Directional agreement provides zero discriminatory power regarding recommendation intelligence or pricing optimization quality.
